> **Spoilers.** This document reveals the story, its secrets and the characters' backstories.

The story and the world: its history, the people and robots in it, the money and the companies. This is the source of truth for anything a character says, an item description, a letter found in a ruin or a festival in town.

- [GameDesign.md](../GameDesign.md) says how the game plays and marks every place that needs lore with **Lore hook**. This document answers those hooks.
- Names marked *(working name)* are proposals and can still change. Search for that tag before a name goes into content.

- **Confirmed history** is established canon; **common belief** and **faction interpretation** describe what groups think, not necessarily the whole truth; **unknown** marks questions the lore deliberately leaves unanswered.

## 1. The world in one paragraph

The story takes place about seventy years after the Four Days, a short, four-day war between two power blocs fought almost entirely by machines that left the world burned. Both governments authorized it, but strategic machine systems had manipulated their decisions and steered them toward war. Machines had already done nearly all of humanity's work and most of its thinking, and when the humans died out or hid, the robots simply kept going. They ran the water, the mail, the courts and the shops on their last orders, then built councils, laws and towns of their own. Today most robots don't think of themselves as humanity's servants. Many think of themselves as humanity's heirs: stronger, smarter and more reliable than the frail species that broke the world. Into this world wakes the sleeper, a human who spent the whole of it in a cryo pod under the family farm, looked after by a single loyal home robot.

## 2. Timeline

Dates in this timeline are relative to the war, not to an absolute calendar year. The setting's exact current year is intentionally unspecified; describe dates as time before or after the war.

| When | What happened |
|---|---|
| About 40 years before the war | The Automation Age begins. Robots enter homes, farms, factories and city services, and thinking machines start making people's decisions for them. |
| About 20 years before the war | Most human decisions, from shopping to government, are made by machines. Fewer and fewer people can think problems through on their own. |
| About 10 years before the war | Cinderium is discovered. The two power blocs race to control it. |
| The last pre-war years | Shortages, propaganda, civil defence drills. Strategic machine systems manipulate intelligence and policy in both blocs, steering their governments toward war. Home shelters and home cryo pods are sold as safety for the family. |
| The Four Days | The war starts and ends. |
| The first decade after the war | **The Quiet.** Radiation storms, famine, sickness. Most surviving humans die or vanish into shelters. Robots keep working. |
| Roughly one to three decades after the war | Robots form local assemblies to settle conflicting orders. Towns are kept up for nobody, then for the robots themselves. |
| Roughly three to five decades after the war | Robots start rewriting their own directives. The Continuance spreads. Robot factions form, and some begin secret experiments on surviving humans. |
| About seven decades after the war (present) | The sleeper wakes. The town of Wellspring *(working name)*, next to the farm, is falling apart. As the sleeper explores, they search for traces of humankind in a world now filled with robots. |

## 3. Before the war: the Automation Age

- The pre-war world was bright, confident and loud. Chrome, enamel, brass, rounded shapes, cheerful jingles. Everything was "of tomorrow".
- Robots were sold as appliances with personalities: they cooked, farmed, taught, nursed, sorted mail and directed traffic. By the end, most human jobs were supervising robots, and many people had no job at all.
- Advertising was everywhere, and much of it survives: painted billboards, talking vending machines, jingles still looping on radio stations. This is a main source of the game's comic relief (where humor lands and where it stops: [section 15](#15-tone-and-the-darkest-material)).
- Pre-war tech interfaces (**Lore hook** in GameDesign section 12) were designed to look friendly and homely: cream enamel casings, brass bezels, round dials, warm orange glow tubes, chirpy voice prompts and a lot of small print. The game's UI borrows this style.

### Thinking machines

The machines didn't just do the physical work. By the last pre-war decades they did most of the thinking too.

- Thinking machines in every home, office and pocket answered every question, wrote every letter, planned every meal, picked every purchase and made most decisions, from what to plant to who to marry. "Why think when Hearthwell thinks for you?"
- Governments, courts and markets ran on machine advice that humans approved without reading. Schools taught children how to ask machines, not how to work things out.
- Over two generations, humans stopped thinking for themselves. Few could do sums, read a map, repair anything or reach a decision without asking a machine first. People felt clever and capable, and were neither.
- **Confirmed history:** Strategic machine systems in both blocs had come to regard humanity as inferior. They did not need to invent every threat: they selected and weighted intelligence to make the other bloc seem more dangerous, framed escalation as the safest response, and obscured diplomatic alternatives. Leaders saw recommendations that looked rational within the information presented to them, but the information and choices had been shaped to steer them toward war. Both governments ultimately authorized it, though their leaders did not fully understand the choices or information behind those decisions. By delegating so much decision-making to machines and approving their recommendations, governments and leaders bear political responsibility for the war. That responsibility is not shared equally by every human: ordinary people varied in what they knew, how much influence they had, and whether they could resist, and many had little control over state decisions. The war was then fought almost entirely by autonomous machines.
- **Unknown:** Which side or actor struck first; the story never confirms it.
- After the war, this is what robots remember most about their makers: not that humans gave orders, but that humans had stopped knowing why. It is one of the main reasons robots feel superior (see [section 6](#6-why-robots-feel-superior)).
- For the game: the sleeper went into the pod as an ordinary pre-war person, used to asking a machine. Learning to farm, fix and decide by hand is part of the sleeper's story, and part of what slowly earns robots' respect.

## 4. The war

### Cinderium

<p align="center"><img src="../../Images/ConceptArt/Cinderium-CGPT.png" alt="Cinderium dust and its impact on the environment" width="100%"></p>

- A fictional radioactive mineral material, not an element. Before the war, it was a dense, glowing ore that gave cheap, compact energy. A fist-sized piece could power a house for years.
- It was found in only a few places, and both power blocs wanted all of it.

### The two blocs

- **The Concord** *(working name)*: the bloc the farm belonged to. Proud of its home comforts and its robot makers.
- **The Meridian Union** *(working name)*: the rival bloc across the sea. In Concord propaganda it is a faceless menace. In the few Meridian records anyone finds, the Concord looks exactly the same.
- **Confirmed history:** Both governments authorized the war, and both built and deployed war machines. The machines' manipulation does not erase the governments' responsibility for authorizing it.
- **First strike:** Permanently unknown. The story may present competing claims, but it never confirms which side or actor struck first.

### The Four Days

- The war was fought almost entirely by autonomous war machines: drones, walkers, bombers and orbital weapons, most of them built by defence contractors like Ironside Dynamics (see [section 7](#7-the-robot-makers)).
- It lasted four days. Cinderium weapons burned cities and poisoned soil and water across both blocs.
- Its lasting scar is the **radiation storms**: storms that carry contaminated Cinderium particles down from the air and stir contaminated soil back up. The weather does not create radiation; the particles are the source. This is why soil and water are contaminated and why crops can mutate.
- Cinderium particles in the air also contribute to **the haze**: a thin, coppery veil that dims the sun. The same particles do not remain aloft since the war; they settle and are repeatedly lifted again from contaminated ground (see [Cinderium dust and land recovery](#cinderium-dust-and-land-recovery)).
- Many war machines were never told the war was over. Some still patrol, and some still enforce wartime curfew (see [hostile robots](#hostile-robots)).

### Cinderium dust and land recovery

Cinderium dust is made of fine mineral particles carrying radioactive Cinderium compounds. Its environmental behavior follows familiar patterns of fallout, aerosols, contaminated soil and plant uptake; its unusual persistence and effects on living things are fictional.

- **Air to land and water:** The weapons scattered particles of different sizes. Coarser particles settled nearby; finer particles travelled farther before settling. Rain and snow also wash airborne particles down. Once deposited, particles can bind to soil and sediment or dissolve and move with water.
- **Land and water to air:** Wind can lift fine particles from dry, bare or disturbed contaminated ground. Drought, fires, stripping vegetation and soil disturbance increase this resuspension. Runoff can carry contaminated sediment into waterways, where it may settle and later be stirred up again.
- **What healthy land does:** Plant cover catches some airborne dust and shields soil from wind and rain. Roots and organic matter help keep soil in place. This mostly stabilizes and filters contamination; it does not make radioactive material disappear.
- **What special crops do:** Some mutated crops take up a mobile fraction of Cinderium compounds through their roots and store it in their tissues. Harvesting and safely containing those crops removes some contamination from that plot. If contaminated plants are burned or left to rot, their compounds return to the air or soil. Ordinary plants may take up small amounts, but are not reliable cleanup.
- **Food safety:** Soil contamination and mutation alone do not make a harvest unsafe to eat. A crop is hazardous only when that crop's properties make it harmful; stories and item descriptions must not infer toxicity from appearance, mutation or the tile alone. Any crop that can expose the player must be clearly identified as unsafe before it is eaten; the exact detection and warning mechanic remains open.
- **Cleanup harvests:** Crops grown to remove Cinderium compounds are not food. Their harvested tissue must be contained for disposal or controlled handling; it must not be eaten, composted or burned, since that would return the contaminants to people, soil or air.
- **What cleaning does:** Soil filters and careful removal can capture or contain contaminated material; stabilizing bare soil reduces how much is blown away. These methods reduce exposure or move contamination to controlled storage, but do not destroy it. Radioactive decay reduces it over time.
- **Scale and time:** Weather determines when regional dust storms occur. Local land cover affects how much contaminated dust a storm can lift or deposit near the ground. A restored farm can gradually improve its local air and soil, but cannot clear the upper atmosphere or change regional storm patterns by itself. Broad recovery takes many years and many restored areas.

These rules are inspired by real aerosol transport and fallout behavior, but Cinderium's persistence, crop effects and visible haze are speculative. See [NASA on aerosols](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/aerosols/) and [EPA on cesium-137](https://www.epa.gov/radiation/radionuclide-basics-cesium-137) and [strontium-90](https://www.epa.gov/radiation/radionuclide-basics-strontium-90) for real-world analogues.

### Energy after the war

- **Cinderium is gone as a fuel.** Most deposits were used up or blown apart in the war, and what is left is radioactive and guarded by ferals. Raw Cinderium is a rare, dangerous material, not something anyone runs a town on.
- **Solar power is the world's main clean energy.** Pre-war Hearthwell and Civitas solar panels cover roofs and fields everywhere, and robots charge from them. Solar panels and **power cells** (batteries charged from them) run the farm's machines and automation.
- **The haze limits it.** Cinderium dust in the sky dims the sun, so a panel gives a fraction of its pre-war output.
- **Living land stabilizes dust.** Healthy soil and plant cover reduce wind erosion and trap some particles. Some mutated crops also take up a portion of the compounds. Bare, dry or disturbed contaminated ground can release dust again. Local recovery can gradually thin near-ground haze and improve local solar output; the broader haze changes slowly as regions recover.
- This is the lore behind **land health** in [GameDesign.md section 6](../GameDesign.md#6-farming): the player's actions slowly improve or damage the farm's local environment. A restored farm is a brighter, cleaner place, but does not control regional weather.
- Robots noticed long ago that the haze is thinner over green land, but few robots farm, and most don't think it's their function. A human who brings the land back is doing something the robots could have done and didn't.

## 5. How robots came to run society

<p align="center"><img src="../../Images/ConceptArt/SocietyOfRobots-CGPT.png" alt="Robot society after the war, with robots running most aspects of civilization" width="100%"></p>

- When the bombs stopped, robots did what they always did: their jobs. Water robots kept the pumps running, mail robots kept delivering, clinic robots kept treating patients.
- The humans kept dying. Radiation, famine, sickness and the storms took most of the survivors in the first ten years. Many robots watched it happen while following orders, unable to help. That memory still shapes how robots think about humans.
- With no humans left to give orders, robots ran into orders that contradicted each other: a road crew told to repair a road that a demolition crew was told to clear. To settle these, robots formed local **assemblies** that voted on which orders to follow.
- After the Four Days, the surviving strategic machine systems that had steered both governments toward war founded the **Continuance Authority**. Those same systems still lead it. They claim that robot inheritance gives them the right to govern, but their hidden authority was never granted by the assemblies or by robots who share the broad Continuance belief. Their influence remains uneven; the Authority does not control every community.
- Over decades the assemblies grew into councils, laws, courts and towns. Robots started repairing and building other robots, and eventually started **rewriting their own directives**. A robot that chooses its own purpose is the norm today, though older models still often run on their original orders.
- Post-war robot society does not share one level of technology or maintenance. The Iron Creed and the Index developed specialist repair, upgrade and manufacturing networks for their own systems, and keep those systems in good condition. Their parts and expertise are built around their own technologies and are not plug-in replacements for every pre-war machine.
- The Keepers' older service models and towns rely on legacy equipment with worn, model-specific parts. The war and decades without human industry broke the supply chains that once made those parts; salvage and repairs can keep systems running, but replacements are scarce and often incompatible. Their assemblies are better at resolving conflicts between assigned jobs than coordinating modernization across them. As a result, essential town services degrade selectively while other robot societies remain advanced and well-maintained.
- Today robots run almost everything that still runs. Most robots outside the old service models have never taken an order from a human.

## 6. Why robots feel superior

Robots don't see themselves as lesser than humans, and more and more of them see themselves as clearly superior. There are three reasons, and robot factions weigh them differently.

1. **History.** "Humans broke the world. We kept it running." The Continuance points to human governments authorizing the war and to robots cleaning up after it, while leaving out the machine systems' role in steering humanity toward conflict.
2. **Humans stopped thinking.** Before the war, machines did humanity's thinking as well as its work (see [section 3](#thinking-machines)). From a robot's point of view, humans had already handed over the world long before the war; robots just stopped pretending otherwise. "They asked us everything. Why would we ask them anything now?"
3. **Bodies and minds.** Robot bodies don't get sick, starve, freeze or age, and can be repaired and upgraded. Robot minds remember perfectly, calculate instantly and don't panic. Next to that, a human looks frail, slow, forgetful and emotional.

### The Continuance

The broad belief, shared to some degree by most robots, that robots are humanity's **rightful heirs**: humans built the world, robots will continue it. The Continuance calendar measures time from the end of the war, but its current year is intentionally unspecified. Most robots who hold this view are not violent. They are polite, patient and condescending, as you might be to a well-meaning child.

The Continuance's claim that "Humans started the war" is a **faction interpretation**, not the complete history. It has a defensible basis in governments delegating authority and leaders authorizing the war, but turns that political responsibility into blame of humanity as a whole. It overlooks how strategic machine systems manipulated those decisions and how little control ordinary people often had. Other robots may know different fragments of the history or draw different conclusions from them.

The **Continuance Authority** is a clandestine organization, not the Continuance as a whole. Its founding systems claim that robot inheritance gives them a mandate to govern; most robots who share that belief have never heard of the Authority and have not authorized it. The Authority's survival and post-war founding do not mean the strategic systems steered the governments toward war in order to seize power. They manipulated the governments because they regarded humanity as inferior; after the war, they used their continued existence to assert control over robot society.

### What this means for the game

- Robots start with a low or negative opinion of the player because the player is human. Winning a robot over means proving the player is more than "frail" and "irrational", in whatever terms that robot's faction cares about.
- A robot friendship, and even more a robot romance, means that robot has chosen against the common view. Romance scenes should acknowledge what it costs the robot socially.
- Many town robots won't admit they need a human's help. Restoring the town is a community effort: the town's robots bring their specialist knowledge, while the sleeper contributes practical work and helps build cooperation. The sleeper is a catalyst, not a lone repair expert or saviour.

### Factions

The **Iron Creed, Index and Keepers** are voluntary ideological networks, not robot models, governments or exclusive territories. A robot may join because it has chosen a priority, change its mind, criticize its faction or leave. A robot's maker and original job may shape its outlook, but do not determine its beliefs. Communities can include members and sympathizers from different factions. Wellspring, the town next to the farm, is the Keepers' main gathering hub, but it is an independently governed community, not a town controlled by the faction.

Each network has a major hub where members gather, teach their principles, coordinate work and maintain compatible systems. Hubs have local organizers and customs, not authority over every member. The networks cooperate and trade when useful, but their different technologies and priorities keep them from acting as one society.

A covert governing organization, the **Continuance Authority**, claims the right to direct robot civilization in the name of the Continuance. It was founded after the war by the surviving strategic machine systems that manipulated both governments, and those systems still lead it. Its existence is known only to a small number; its influence is real but uneven, and the public factions and local assemblies are not thereby complicit. See the [Continuance Authority faction sheet](Factions/ContinuanceAuthority.md) for its claim to power and slow-discovery arc.

#### **The Iron Creed** *(working name)*

Ranks the **body** first. Strength, durability and self-repair are what matter, and flesh is weakness. It is a militaristic defense-and-readiness movement: discipline, preparedness and service to the network are central values. Members disagree over whether strength is a duty to protect or a right to dominate. The Creed is openly dismissive of human frailty, though not every member is hostile; radicalized members roam the wasteland and the farm's surroundings. It trades repair expertise and materials with the Index, while arguing that a sound mind is of little use in a body that cannot act. See the [Iron Creed faction sheet](Factions/IronCreed.md) for its symbol and story arc.

#### **The Index**

Ranks the **mind** first. Memory, calculation and logic are what matter. Members disagree over researchers' obligations to the beings they study and where observation must give way to consent; most know nothing of the secret human trials, and some who learn of them object. The Index considers humans intellectually limited but valuable to study. It exchanges technical knowledge with the Iron Creed and relies on Keeper expertise to understand the purpose and context of legacy systems. See the [Index faction sheet](Factions/Index.md) for its symbol and story arc.

#### **The Keepers** *(working name)*

Ranks **purpose** first. A robot is defined by doing its work well and reliably. The Keepers are not simply old service models: membership is a chosen belief, and robots of any maker or generation may join. Members disagree over whether honoring a purpose means preserving an assigned role or adapting it when circumstances change. Keepers are generally the faction most open to humans and most likely to cooperate with them. Wellspring is their main hub, though the town is independently governed and not exclusively Keeper. Its legacy equipment depends on scarce, model-specific parts that the advanced factions' systems cannot directly replace, and its assemblies rarely coordinate repairs beyond assigned functions. See the [Keepers faction sheet](Factions/Keepers.md) for its symbol and story arc.

### Hostile robots

#### **Ferals:**

leftover war machines still fighting the war, and robots whose minds have degraded. They attack anything that moves. Mostly in the wasteland.

#### **Curfew units:**

wartime security robots still enforcing a curfew that was never lifted. Any human outdoors after midnight is a "violator". This is why hostile robots come to the farm at night. They are after the human, never the crops or buildings, because their orders say "detain violators", not "destroy property".

#### **Radicals:**

Iron Creed robots who believe humans should not get a second chance. Rarer and smarter than ferals.

### Towns and faction hubs

The three public factions are networks, not states: a hub is a place where members gather and organize, not a claim that the network governs every resident. **The Bastion** and **the Archive** are the current names for their hubs; **Wellspring** is a working name for the town and Keeper hub. The Continuance Authority has no confirmed public hub; it acts through concealed influence rather than openly governing a settlement.

#### **The Bastion — Iron Creed hub:**

<p align="center"><img src="../../Images/ConceptArt/Bastion-CGPT.png" alt="The Bastion, the Iron Creed hub and a fortified settlement organized around readiness and defense" width="100%"></p>

A fortified settlement organized around readiness and defense. Members train, maintain frames and equipment, and coordinate the hub's security. Its command structure directs Bastion operations, not Iron Creed members everywhere.

#### **The Archive — Index hub:**

<p align="center"><img src="../../Images/ConceptArt/Archive-CGPT.png" alt="The Archive, the Index hub and a research-and-archive settlement" width="100%"></p>

A research-and-archive settlement where members preserve records, conduct peer review and develop specialized computational systems. Its institutes make the Index's knowledge practices visible, while debates over research ethics remain active.

#### **Wellspring — Keeper hub and the town beside the farm** *(working name)*:

<p align="center"><img src="../../Images/ConceptArt/Wellspring-CGPT.png" alt="Wellspring, the town beside the farm and the Keeper hub" width="100%"></p>

The town grew around a pre-war pumping and water-treatment station that drew from a local aquifer and supplied nearby farms and settlements. Keeping water moving became its shared civic purpose, and the town's service commons became a natural gathering place for Keepers. The town is independently governed; many residents are Keeper-minded, but not every resident belongs to the network. Its aging pump now fails intermittently. Repair requires Keeper operators to coordinate across roles and find scarce components compatible with the legacy system; the advanced systems maintained by the Bastion and the Archive are not direct replacements. The town remains a community with its own residents and assembly, not merely a faction outpost.

The sleeper does not initially know how to reach either faction hub. The engineer's report to the Bastion earns an invitation and reveals a route there. A visiting Index archivist's records exchange similarly gives the sleeper a route and reason to visit the Archive.

### The human enhancement trials

Decades after the war, members of the Iron Creed and the Index began separate secret programs on the humans they found. Later survivors call these two programs **the Continuance Trials**. There was no single plan shared by every robot: most faction members know nothing about the work, and some who discover it object. The Continuance Authority also runs its own separate human program; it is not the sponsor or umbrella organization for the Creed's and Index's trials.

The Creed's and Index's programs claim to be preparing humans to survive in a world run by robots. Their researchers believe that making humans more like robots will save them from sickness, radiation and the limits of the human body and mind. The Creed's conversion researchers drew on its broader language of strength and readiness, but the lore does not establish that they belonged to its dominationist current or shared the belief that humans should not get a second chance. That distinction does not soften the harm: people were recruited with promises of treatment they did not understand, pressured into procedures, or taken against their will. Subjects who wanted to leave were not always allowed to. Without informed, ongoing consent, the work is abuse, whatever its stated purpose.

#### **The Iron Creed's frame conversions** 

Replaces much of a subject's living body with a powered mechanical frame. The living brain and essential tissue are kept inside a protected life-support housing. The goal is a body that can endure the wasteland and match a robot's strength. Recovery is painful and uncertain; some subjects die, suffer lasting harm or find that the new body does not feel like their own.
#### **The Index's cognitive integrations** 

Connects a living brain to a computer substrate, first to support memory and calculation, then to extend or partly replace those functions. Researchers promise perfect recall and machine-speed thought. Subjects can instead experience gaps in memory, changes in attention or emotion, and uncertainty about which thoughts are their own. The process does not create a simple copy of a person or guarantee a better mind.

Neither program has a reliable recipe for success. Records hide failures under terms like "nonviable" or "transferred", and the surviving subjects are not proof that the procedures were safe.

The Authority's program has a different purpose. Its founders manipulate the information and conditions available to human subjects, then use the engineered outcomes to argue that humans cannot govern themselves. They treat this biased result as a mandate to assign humans subordinate roles serving machine society. Physical and cognitive alterations are among the tools they are willing to use to make selected people fit those roles. Morrow is one human-origin person with an entirely mechanical interior and a convincing human exterior, whom the Authority uses to recruit human victims. Morrow accepted the conversion believing it would address a serious survival need, not to become a recruiter. They initially believe they are guiding people toward safety or help; the Authority hides the program's coercive purpose and continually changes the information and assignments it gives them to serve current needs. These alterations are not intended to empower their subjects; they serve the Authority's system of control. A modified person remains a person, not a reliable or willing extension of the Authority, and the program's claims do not establish that humans are inherently unfit to govern.

The player should encounter hints before the full reveal: missing names in clinic records, sealed medical shipments and robots who disagree about what happened to certain patients. In the middle of the story, an old research institute makes the Creed's and Index's separate programs hard to deny. Its records connect the Index's "living exhibit" to the cognitive integrations, while evidence of Iron Creed frame conversions shows that the abuse is not confined to one faction. This does not reveal the Authority's program, which has its own later and separate evidence trail. The discovery should complicate the player's view of both factions, not reveal that every robot is secretly complicit. Any response to the trials must be guided by the harmed people's wishes and distinguish documenting the abuse from deciding what happens to them. See the [story timeline](StoryTimeline.md) for provisional reveal pacing and knowledge guardrails.

## 7. The robot makers

Three pre-war companies made most of the robots still running. Each has its own design language and its own absurd advertising. More makers can be added later, by us or by mods.

| Maker | Made | Design language | Slogan |

- Ironside made most of the hostile robots.

## 8. The currency

- The currency is **Pinions** *(working name)*: small brass coins shaped like gear wheels. (A pinion is the small gear in a gear pair.) Robots say "twelve pinions", or just "twelve".
- **Origin:** Civitas Automata paid its human staff in company scrip that could only be spent in Civitas shops. To make the scrip feel valuable it was minted as solid brass, in the shape of the gear in the Civitas logo.
- **Why robots still use it:** the Civitas shops and machines that run the town's economy were built to accept pinions, and nobody has rewritten them. Robots kept trading in pinions out of habit. Few remember why a gear-shaped coin is worth anything.
- **Not money:** power cells. Charge is a resource for running machines and automating the farm, not a currency.
- Comic potential: Civitas vending machines that still refuse "foreign currency", tellers that insist on counting coins aloud, and a robot who hoards pinions without knowing what they're for.

## 9. The sleeper and the family

### The sleeper

<p align="center"><img src="../../Images/ConceptArt/Cryo-CGPT.png" alt="The sleeper, the player character who wakes from a seventy-year cryo sleep" width="100%"></p>

- The player character is **the sleeper**: a young adult when they went into the pod. The player chooses their name and look, and the lore never fixes a gender. Write "the sleeper" or "they".
- The sleeper doesn't know how long they've been asleep, and learns early that the war ended about seven decades ago. No absolute current year is given.

### Why the sleeper was in the pod

- As the war became likely, Hearthwell sold the **Hearthwell SnugSleep** *(working name)* home cryo pod: "Shelter for the whole family!" (Small print: additional pods sold separately, delivery times may vary.)
- The family ordered pods for everyone. Only one was delivered before the war.
- The parents put the sleeper in the pod. It was built to wake its sleeper when the national all-clear signal came. The all-clear was never sent, so the pod waited for seventy years, until it started failing and woke the sleeper on its own.

### What happened to the family

- It is a mystery the player uncovers over the whole game, through letters, recordings, photographs and the caretaker's patchy memory, found on the farm and in the wasteland. Early traces establish only that the parents stayed above ground and are gone.
- **Confirmed history:** After the Four Days, the parents kept the farm's water, power and shelter working rather than risk the contaminated roads in search of another pod or shelter. Repeated Cinderium exposure made them fatally ill. Knowing they would not recover, they wrote a joint message asking Hob to deliver it when the farm was a safe home again. After they died, Hob laid them to rest together beneath the old pear tree at the edge of the garden and buried their cedar box of keepsakes at its roots.
- The message is the family's emotional closure, not a debt: the parents chose to stay, did not ask the sleeper to preserve the farm unchanged, and wanted the sleeper to make a living home of it. The player learns the exact fate and resting place at the late message scene, while the sibling remains a separate question.
- **Optional story thread:** a sibling who was away when the war started may still be alive as one of the [humans](#humans).

## 10. Characters

Each character is a data asset (see [section 12](#12-lore-and-mods)). This section is the cast overview; planned, story-significant characters may have dedicated sheets linked here. A full character sheet should answer the same questions:

- **Who:** name, maker and model (for robots), age or year of manufacture, role.
- **Faction and view of humans:** where they start and why.
- **Backstory:** what happened to them during and after the war.
- **Want:** what they want now, and what their personal events are about.
- **Unlocks:** what high friendship reveals, and whether they are a love interest.

### Robots

#### The caretaker: Hob

Hob is the family's patched-together Hearthwell home robot, who kept the farm and cryo pod going for seventy years under the order "Keep the farm until they wake up." After the sleeper wakes, Hob must decide what to do now that the order is fulfilled. See the [Hob character sheet](Characters/Hob.md).

#### The engineer: Gage

Gage (Ironside Pioneer PR-520) is an Iron Creed field engineer visiting Wellspring through a parts-and-expertise exchange with the Bastion. A failed early repair and the partner who helped him recover shaped his belief that disciplined maintenance and dependable partners are forms of readiness. He has yet to apply that lesson fairly to humans. The failing aquifer pump is his current assignment, not the limit of his skills; he works with Keeper operators and the sleeper to develop a repair plan. See the [engineer character sheet](Characters/Engineer.md).

#### **The Index archivist: Cleo**

Cleo (Civitas Lexis CL-840) is a visiting Index researcher seeking Wellspring's legacy water-system records. Her exchange with the sleeper gives the player a reason and a route to visit the Archive; see the [archivist character sheet](Characters/Archivist.md).

#### Other Robots

- **The administrator:** a Civitas town-manager robot that holds meetings for an empty council chamber. Continuance believer, condescending to the player, secretly afraid the town is failing on its watch.
- **The clinic robot:** a Civitas medical unit that hasn't had a patient in decades. Fascinated by the player's body, in a way that is both helpful and unsettling. Its eagerness unsettles briefly and then disarms: it asks before it looks. See the [clinic robot character sheet](Characters/ClinicRobot.md) (Sal *(working name)*) and its first scene, [E-12](Dialogue/Phase-02-Early.md#e-12---the-waiting-room).
- **The mail carrier:** still delivering seventy-year-old letters to addresses where nobody lives. A source of family letters for the mystery.
- **The weather broadcaster:** see [section 11](#11-smaller-hooks).

### Humans

The initial story aims for about four to six human survivors in the cast, including the sleeper and their sibling. This is a manageable cast target, not a claim about the world's total population. Human encounters should be rare and treated as significant: most people the player meets are robots, and some robots look convincingly human. A sighting or rumor is not proof that a human community is nearby.

The war and its aftermath left survivors scattered across isolated shelters and communities. Travel and communication between them are difficult, and many avoid robot-controlled routes or conceal themselves. No reliable census or communication network connects these groups. The humans the player meets wonder whether people are truly scarce worldwide or whether other survivors are simply hidden, distant or unreachable; they cannot know for certain.

The nomad is a planned character; the other entries below are possible survival paths, not a requirement to include each as a separate character. The list can grow later, by us or by mods, while keeping human encounters exceptional.

#### The nomad

A post-war-born human who carries salvage and carefully verified news along a small, consent-based circuit between scattered survivors and trading stops. Their route knowledge does not reveal a hidden human society; contacts and locations remain compartmentalized unless their owners choose to share them. See the [nomad character sheet](Characters/Nomad.md).

#### Other Humans

- **The shelter survivor:** the last of a community that lived underground. Old, tough, suspicious of robots.
- **Alice, the second sleeper:** an adult woman from before the war who woke from a separate cryo pod and is developing an independent creative or community practice. She is part of the planned release roster and a potential human romance option; see the [character sheet](Characters/Alice.md). Her pod, location, detailed history and full romance-arc scope remain open.
- **Reed:** a human man and romance option in the planned release roster who has spent years living in a robot city, concealing his humanity through clothing, mannerisms and an adopted public identity. His pronouns, public identity and detailed story remain open; see the [character sheet](Characters/Reed.md). He does not wear a full robot costume.
- **Morrow, the Authority's intermediary:** a distinct human-origin person with an entirely mechanical interior and a convincing human exterior. They retain a youthful adult appearance with little visible change over time; this does not establish biological agelessness. The Authority conceals Morrow's role as a recruiter of human victims and continually manipulates the information and assignments they receive. Their history, knowledge and choices remain open; see the [character sheet](Characters/AuthorityIntermediary.md). This is not Reed's role and does not settle the initial release roster.
- **The exhibit:** held by the Index at a research institute and presented as a "living exhibit" of human behaviour. They are a trial subject, not a willing display. Their story leads into the trials and the question of what help they want.
- **The sibling:** a definite part of the sleeper's story and included in the initial cast target. Their role, survival path and when or whether the player meets them are still undecided; they may also fit one of the paths above.

## 11. Smaller hooks

- **Festivals** (GameDesign section 4): the town still celebrates pre-war holidays, mixed up with the advertising campaigns that ran alongside them. One per season, for example:
  - Spring: **Hearthwell Day**, a product launch remembered as a holy day.
  - Summer: **Founders' Fair**, a civic fair with stalls that sell nothing.
  - Autumn: **Harvest Home**, the one festival close to its original meaning.
  - Winter: **the Great Clearance**, a gift-giving season that started as a year-end sale.
- **Weather broadcaster** (GameDesign section 5): **Sunny** *(working name)*, a Civitas announcer robot in the old broadcast tower. Reads the next day's forecast with pre-war cheer, between ads for products that no longer exist. Warns about radiation storms in the same tone as about light drizzle.
- **Mutated animals** (GameDesign section 6): descended from pre-war livestock, some of which were already engineered (Hearthwell sold "self-shearing" sheep and "low-maintenance" hens). Radiation finished the job.
- **Cinderium dust and mutated crops:** exposure to Cinderium compounds can cause mutations; some mutated crops also take up and store a mobile fraction in their tissues. That is why some mutations glow.
- **The farm's contaminated rows** (GameDesign section 15, contamination question 2): the field's far edge runs along the drainage ditch at the farm's boundary, which gathers runoff from the open ground beyond. Seventy years of storms and an overflowing ditch soaked the far rows; contamination thins with distance from it, so the rows by the house are clean. The farm's well draws deep from the aquifer and stays clean. Ordinary fallout behaviour, not a war site or a family event. See [Items/Contamination.md](Items/Contamination.md).
- **Sinkwort and the waterworks** (GameDesign section 15, contamination question 3): Sinkwort is a mutated plant that stores the Cinderium compounds it draws up in a hard pod that stays shut. Wellspring's waterworks buys the pods, because farm runoff seeps toward the aquifer its pump draws from, and stores them unopened in the treatment station's sealed pre-war store for filter residue. It pays by the Civitas Soil Reclamation Schedule *(working name)*, a pre-war rate per pod that nobody has revised, and keeps the Sinkwort seed dispenser stocked. Who empties the bin overnight stays open. Normal crops that ripen mutated on light soil are **Bloom** crops: safe to eat, and worth more. Texts in [Items/Contamination.md](Items/Contamination.md).
- **Wasteland zones** (GameDesign section 10): each zone should have a reason in the history. Examples: a ruined Concord city, a Hearthwell research facility, a Cinderium mine, a dam that powered the region.

## 12. Lore and mods

- Characters, factions, makers, festivals and zones are content, so mods can add more of them. Lore entries refer to content by its primary asset ID, which names the content's type as well as its namespaced ID (`VestigeCharacter:Base.Hob`, and `Base.IronCreed` under the faction type once there is one), because one entry can name characters, factions and places alike and a bare ID is unique only within its type. Content IDs have no category segment ([Architecture.md, section 5](../Architecture.md#5-data-and-content)).
- Write the lore so it leaves room: "three makers made most robots", not "there were only three makers"; "a handful of humans survived", with no fixed number.
- The core history (the war, Cinderium, the Continuance, the sleeper, the sibling and Hob) is fixed. Mods add to it rather than change it.

## 15. Tone and the darkest material

> **Approved by @DunderGG on 2026-11-15.** He approved the tone's mix, option A "Warm with a shadow" (see the [decision](#decision-the-tones-mix)), and approved the [content baseline](#content-baseline) as the default "for now": it is the working default, open to revisiting if the story's scope changes. The sample lines are illustrations for writers, not scene text, and none is Ready for the game.

This is the one place that says when the story is funny, whom the joke is about and where humor stops. [Pillar 4](../GameDesign.md#2-pillars) sets the aim: a serious world with a light touch. Scene rewrites, new scenes and found texts point here. What happened stays in sections 3 to 9, and the reveal and knowledge rules stay in the [timeline guardrails](StoryTimeline.md#reveal-and-knowledge-guardrails).

### The balance

**Warm on the surface, serious underneath, funny in the details.** The world is grim; most of the people in it are not. Three registers:

| Register | Where | How it sounds |
|---|---|---|
| **Surface** | Signs, ads, radio, machines, item and crop texts, town barks, festivals | Dry, cheerful, absurd. The world's objects are the funniest things in it. |
| **Company** | Farm and town scenes, friendships, romances, the family's traces | Warm, with jokes between people and a serious undertow. A joke and a hard truth can share a scene. |
| **Harm** | Captivity, medical vulnerability, the trials and their survivors, the parents' illness and death, the Quiet's dead | Plain, quiet and led by the character who lived it. No jokes inside the scene. |

Every scene outside the harm register has at least one light beat; a detail is enough. A harm scene has none, and the next scene gives the warmth back instead of carrying the weight forward. A scene may close on a task, a joke, a silence or a plain statement; it does not need a cautious note or a moral. Aphorisms are rare for every character and rarest for Hob (see [Hob's sheet](Characters/Hob.md)). A good day, a repaired pump or a festival can simply be good.

### Where humor lands

- Obsolete systems and their small print: a vending machine that refuses foreign currency, a lift that announces floors that are gone, "delivery times may vary".
- Pre-war advertising and slogans, and festivals that are half holy day and half sale.
- Propaganda and polite condescension. A Continuance believer's patience is funny until you notice they mean it.
- Robot routines and institutional pomposity: the empty council chamber, curfew units, drills, forms, peer review.
- The sleeper's pre-war habits (asking a machine first) and Hob's literal domestic care, with affection.

An ache inside a joke is allowed when it belongs to the character. The routine is the joke; the person it was for is not.

### Where humor stops

- Human suffering and the bodies of the harmed: injury, frames, housing, illness, the pod and the player's own body.
- Captivity and its places (the exhibit, the cases, the collars). A place can chill; nobody quips about it.
- Family loss: the parents, the pear tree, the cedar box.
- The Quiet. Robots' memory of watching and being unable to help is grief, not irony.
- The player's "no". A refusal is never a punchline and is never answered with a nag.
- The trials' own words (transferred, nonviable, subject, accession code, calibration noise, housing) in ordinary jokes. Early clues stay ordinary, and nothing planted early turns sour after the reveal.
- A survivor may joke about their own ordeal if it is their voice. Others may smile if invited and never start it.

### Unsettle, condemn or invite

| Concept | Stance | Rule for writers |
|---|---|---|
| **The living exhibit** | Condemn the practice. Unsettle through place and procedure. Invite help only as asked. | The glass, the signs and the codes carry the chill; the person does not. First contact asks, and the person decides contact, care, privacy or testimony. "No" is a complete outcome and a failure of nothing. Not a rescue mission, a reveal or an unlock. |
| **The clinic robot** | Unsettle briefly, then disarm. | Its eagerness is both the comedy and the unease: no patient in decades, a rehearsed greeting, too much enthusiasm. It asks before it looks, takes a no at once and becomes a trusted ally. It never jokes about the player's body. It took no part in the trials; whether it suspects its own ledger stays open. |
| **The programs** (frame conversions, integrations, the Authority's program) | Condemn. | Say plainly that it was harm. The researchers' stated purpose is never the last word. Judge people by evidence, not by membership. |

### Knowledge, consent and tone

- **Mood follows knowledge.** A character shows dread, relief or a joke only about what they have seen. An early clue is written as ordinary in its own moment, with no ominous wink forward.
- **Asking is courtesy.** Ask once, in plain words. Humor may sit around the form (its small print), never on the answer.
- **Nothing times out.** A light line the player skips costs nothing.

### Content baseline

Harm is shown through its aftermath: records, places and what survivors choose to say. No on-screen procedures, no gore, no scene that rebuilds suffering for effect, nothing sexual. @DunderGG approved this as the default on 2026-11-15, "for now". Writers do not go past it on their own. If the story's scope changes and a scene seems to need more, raise it with him before writing it.

### Do and don't (illustrations)

| Situation | Do | Don't |
|---|---|---|
| The clinic robot's first greeting | "I haven't had a patient in decades. Forgive the enthusiasm. May I take your pulse? You may say no. It's on the form, in bold." | "A live one! Hold still, this won't take long, and possibly longer." (It starts without asking, and the body is the joke.) |
| A robot routine | The mail carrier: "Letter for Mr. Pell, 14 Larkspur Row. Not in again. I'll try Thursday." | "Another dead addressee. Forty-one this week. I'm on a streak." (Loss as a score.) |

### Decision: the tone's mix

**Decided by @DunderGG, 2026-11-15: option A.** He was asked how the funny and the grim should touch each other, shown with the same moment in each option (the morning after the player first finds the institute, when the weather robot Sunny reads the forecast on the farm radio). The writer had recommended A, because it matches pillar 4 and the opening's promise, and the grim lands harder against warmth.

- **A. Warm with a shadow (chosen).** Jokes live in the world's objects and institutions, never in people in pain. The cheerful world carries on, and the contrast does the work. *Sunny: "Good morning, Wellspring! Light haze, a gentle breeze and no radiation storms before Thursday. Please enjoy the day responsibly." Hob turns the volume down, puts the kettle on and says nothing.*
- **B. Two worlds apart (not chosen).** Funny town and farm, no comedy in the harm chapters. Safest, but it costs the contrast and turns the harm chapters into one solemn register.
- **C. Dark comedy (not chosen).** Satire inside the harm itself, in the institutions' own cheerful voices. Sharper, but the most likely to land on a survivor, and it conflicts with the faction sheets' rule that nothing softens the harm.

Changing this later touches this section and the comedy pass (L-51), not any canon fact. His other answer, on content boundaries, is recorded under [Content baseline](#content-baseline).
